# Is This Load-Bearing?

You want to open up the kitchen. There is a wall in the way, and before anything else can be decided you have to know one thing about it: is it holding the building up?

Nothing about the wall tells you. Plastered and painted, a wall carrying the floor above looks exactly like one put there to divide two rooms. Both are about the same thickness. Both sound the same when you knock. The one difference that matters is invisible from the room you are standing in.

So an engineer does not look at the wall. They look at what rests on it.

Which way do the floor joists run — parallel to the wall, or across it? Do they end on top of it, or pass over? Is there a beam or a foundation wall directly underneath, in line? Is it an exterior wall, in which case the answer is almost certainly yes? None of those questions is about the wall's appearance. Every one of them is about the wall's situation.

That is the first thing worth taking from the building trade, and it is not a figure of speech. **A wall is not load-bearing by nature. It is load-bearing by circumstance.** The same bricks, laid the same way, hold up a house or hold up nothing depending on which direction somebody ran the joists. Turn the floor above ninety degrees and a structural wall becomes a partition without a single brick moving.

## What is actually pressing down

Ask an engineer what a wall carries and you do not get one number. You get a list, because the loads are different in kind and arrive by different routes.

**Dead load** is the permanent weight — the structure itself, the roof, the floors, the pipes and ducts that will still be there in fifty years. It does not change. It is the easiest to calculate and the easiest to forget, because nobody notices a floor.

**Live load** is everything temporary: people, furniture, snow on the roof, a room full of filing cabinets somebody put in without asking. It varies by the hour and by the decade. A building converted from apartments to offices has the same dead load and a different live one.

**Lateral load** arrives sideways rather than downward — wind against the face of the building, ground moving in an earthquake. This is the one people picture last, because gravity is intuitive and horizontal force is not. Plenty of structures that comfortably carry what sits on them fail at what pushes them.

The three are not degrees of the same thing. They are different questions, answered separately, and a wall can be adequate for two and inadequate for the third.

## Loads travel, and the path can be traced

The weight does not stop at the wall. Roofing and flooring rest on joists; joists span between beams; beams carry to girders or columns; columns deliver to the foundation; the foundation spreads it into the soil. Every part is holding up whatever is above it and handing the total to whatever is below.

That chain is the reason the question can be answered at all. You are never guessing about a wall in isolation. You are asking where it sits on a path that starts at the roof and ends in the ground, and the path can be walked, one connection at a time, in either direction.

## Engineering knows what it does not know

There is a number for the uncertainty. A structure is designed to a **factor of safety** — in ordinary American practice, 1.5, meaning it is built to resist one and a half times the load anyone expects it to see. For assessing an existing building against earthquake forces the minimum drops to 1.0, which is a way of saying: we would not build it this way today, and we are not going to demolish it either.

The number is arguable and it is argued about. What matters is that there is one. The margin between what is expected and what is survivable has been written down, so it can be checked, defended, and revised.

## Where the principle stopped paying

Load-bearing masonry has a ceiling, and there is a building that shows exactly where it is.

The Monadnock Building in Chicago is the tallest load-bearing brick building ever put up: sixteen rentable storeys, finished in 1891, standing on walls **six feet thick at the base** and eighteen inches at the top. It is still there. It works. Nothing about it was a mistake.

What it also demonstrated was the limit. Going higher would have meant thicker walls still, and thicker walls eat the ground floor — the space you are building in order to rent. The method did not fail structurally. It stopped being worth what it cost.

The proof sits in the same building. The south half, finished two years later in 1893, was built on a steel frame instead, and it delivered **fifteen percent more rentable space while weighing fifteen percent less.** One address, two structural strategies, and a measurement of the difference. The masonry half is not the wrong answer. It is the answer to a shorter building.

## What software has instead

Everything above is a discipline. There is a definition of load-bearing that can be tested, a taxonomy of loads that separates different questions, a path that can be traced from roof to soil, a margin with a number in it, and a legal obligation to have someone qualified check before anyone opens a wall.

Now consider how the same question reaches you at work.

*You should always program to an interface.* *Business logic doesn't belong in the database.* *Don't repeat yourself.* *Mock your dependencies.* *That should be a Repository.*

Each of those arrives as a finished sentence. None of them says what it is resting on, which loads it was calculated for, what happens to the rest of the structure if you take it out, or how far past its range it stays true. They arrive in the same tone as each other and in the same tone as *dependencies must not be circular*, which is a different kind of statement altogether — one that follows from what a dependency is, and is not going to stop being true because your situation changed.

Some of what you have been told is carrying weight. Some of it is a partition somebody put up in 1998, painted the same colour as the rest. Both are quoted with equal confidence by people of equal seniority, and the confidence tells you nothing, because it is a fact about the speaker rather than about the wall.

The builder can call a structural engineer. There is no equivalent number to ring, which is why the question has to be one you can run yourself.

That is what the rest of this book is for. It is not a list of rules to follow — it is an attempt to supply the missing apparatus: a way of telling what kind of claim you are holding, what it rests on, and where it stops. Each chapter takes one piece of common advice, works out which kind it is, and shows where it stops applying, because a claim whose boundary nobody can find is usually too vague to use.

And every chapter ends the same way: not with a rule, but with the question that does the work for that subject. *Can I change the other side?* *Which fact about our situation would have to change for this to be the wrong choice?* *Whose name is against this, and do they have the most context?* Those are the tools. This one is the general form of all of them, and it is three words long.

Before you knock the wall through: is this load-bearing?

The next chapter starts the apparatus, by sorting the claims you meet into the five kinds this book uses and setting out how much authority each one carries.
